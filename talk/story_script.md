# Shade or Shortcut — talk script for the story (about 7 minutes)

Screen numbers follow the story page (#1 is the title screen). Lines in *italics* are cues; the rest is spoken.
Move one screen at a time (Page Down / arrow key). Wait for the map to settle before speaking over a new map.

---

## Opening

**#1 — Shade or Shortcut** *(the two walks draw themselves)*

On a summer afternoon in Philadelphia, most trips home end on foot: from the train or the bus to the front door.
That last walk is the one part of the trip nobody cools. Our question is simple: on the hottest afternoon of the summer,
how much of that walk can a different street take out of the sun and the heat, and what does it cost in distance?

## 01 · The heat

**#2 — Summer afternoons are hot and bright** *(city surface-temperature map)*

Last summer the airport hit 101 °F on June 24, the hottest day of the summer, and 36 days reached 90 °F or more.
The map is the ground itself, seen by Landsat: on a July morning in 2024, half of the city's surface was already above 48 °C.

**#3 — Follow one** *(the camera moves from the stations to one of them)*

431,114 homes in Philadelphia are within a 15-minute walk of a station or a frequent bus or trolley stop. We follow one walk
through the whole story: from 36th Street Portal in University City to a home 1.1 km away, at 3 pm on June 24.

## 02 · The shade

**#4 — Where shade comes from** *(the three diagrams)*

Shade on a sidewalk comes from three places: trees, buildings, and walking indoors through a building.

**#5 — Mapped on every sidewalk** *(the colours spread over the campus)*

Using the city's 2022 LiDAR scan, we know the height of every building and every tree. Every two metres along
7,237 km of sidewalk, we cast a ray toward the sun and record whether that spot is in sun, tree shade, building shade or indoors.

**#6 — Every sidewalk, every hour** *(the day plays from 8:00 to 19:00)*

And we do it for every hour of the day. Watch the shadows sweep across University City: long in the morning and the
evening, shortest around midday, which is when people most need them.

## 03 · Sun: the shadiest walk

**#7 — Same station, same door** *(the shortest walk draws, then the shadiest)*

Here is our walk. The shortest route is 1,142 m and 88 % of it is in the sun. The shadiest route is only 26 m longer,
but just 24 % of it is in the sun: it avoids 723 m of sunshine. The same trip, two very different experiences.

**#8 — Mostly in the sun** *(city grid)*

Is that typical? Across the city, for walks of 300 to 800 m from frequent stops, 77 % of the shortest walk home is in the sun.

**#9 — No shadier way home** *(the 100 walks in the card)*

But a better route is often not there. Out of 100 walks home, 63 have no shadier option at all. For the rest it can
help a lot: 13 in 100 avoid a quarter of the sun or more. On average the shadiest walk has 7.8 % less sun, for just 0.4 % more distance.

**#10 — Dense grids give a choice** *(clusters; the blue cells stand out)*

Where you have a choice depends on the street grid. In these 51 blue cells a shadier street is usually within reach:
they have about 44 km of sidewalk per square kilometre, against 31 where there is no option. More streets, more choices.

## 04 · Heat: the coolest walk

**#11 — Shade is not the same as cool** *(the felt-temperature map of University City)*

So far shade was a yes or a no. But how hot does a sidewalk actually feel? We ran SOLWEIG, a heat model, at one-metre
resolution across the city. It gives the UTCI: how hot it feels to the body, in degrees. At 3 pm, a sunny sidewalk
feels like 45.9 °C, right at the edge of extreme heat stress. A step into shade takes about 5 degrees off,
and it is still very strong heat stress.

**#12 — Even the shade** *(the day plays; the chart follows the hours)*

Over the day, from 10 am to 5 pm, even the shade stays in very strong heat stress. Shade lowers the strain; it does not remove it.

**#13 — Tree shade is as cool as building shade** *(close-up: three sidewalks a block apart)*

One finding surprised us. Standard shade routing counts a metre under a tree as only half as good as building shade.
In felt temperature it is just as good. Here, a block apart: 46.0 °C in the sun, 40.4 °C under trees, 41.1 °C in building shade.

**#14 — The walk, felt** *(two walkers move at the same pace; the profile draws in the card)*

So we route on felt temperature instead. Two people leave the station together. On the shortest walk, 606 m are in
extreme heat stress; on the coolest walk, only 135 m. The coolest walk is 26 m longer and feels 2.8 °C cooler on average.

**#15 — Across the city** *(city grid of how much cooler)*

Across the city, the coolest walk lowers the felt temperature by about half a degree on average, and takes about a
quarter off the metres in extreme heat, for under 1 % more distance. For 77 % of walks it is the same as the shadiest walk.

## 05 · Indoors

**#16 — Through buildings** *(3D campus; violet buildings are open to walkers)*

On campus there is one more option: going through buildings. When the mapped buildings are open, about one in five
shadiest walks goes indoors for part of the way.

## 06 · Your walk

**#17 — Where shade is missing** *(only the orange cells stay lit)*

And this is the map that matters most. In 322 of 510 cells, the walk home is 80 % in the sun, and 86 % of walks have no
shadier street nearby. A better route cannot help here; shade has to be added, with street trees, awnings and shelters.
If you want to check your own walk, at any hour of either day, the route tool and the city map are one click away.
Thank you.
